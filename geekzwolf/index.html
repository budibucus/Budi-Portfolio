Geekzwolf · Co-founder & Chief Product Officer · Independent venture

A Web3 gaming bet that didn't work — and what it taught me

Between Waresix and Rey.id I took a real swing at a startup of my own. It didn't reach the scale we set out for. I've kept it here, documented directly, because the lessons on timing and product-market fit are worth more than another highlight reel.

The bet

Between my time at Waresix and Rey.id, I made a real bet. Web3 gaming was emerging, and the promise — players truly owning and earning from their in-game assets — looked like a genuine shift in how games could work. I co-founded Geekzwolf to bring that shift to Southeast Asian gamers. As Co-founder and Chief Product Officer, I set the product vision, built a co-investing platform where gamers and investors pooled resources into gaming guilds, and led our push to onboard Web3 game studios into Indonesia specifically, on a bet that local market entry could be our differentiator.

The promise, and the harsh reality

The market turned out to be ahead of the ecosystem. Most game studios we engaged with were building gaming tokens, not good gameplay — they hadn't cracked how to make games people wanted to play for fun, not just token rewards. We could generate interest, but long-term engagement was hard to sustain when the games themselves weren't keeping players around.

The token crash

Then came the second blow: gaming token prices collapsed. Geekzwolf's co-investment platform worked well when token prices were high. As prices crashed, the entire co-investing model became irrelevant almost overnight. The two problems fed each other — without compelling games, engagement leaned on token rewards; when token prices crashed, the games and the business model lost their reason to exist at the same moment.

Onboarding Web3 studios to Indonesia: a tough road

One core strategy was bringing Web3 game studios into Indonesia's gaming market. In practice, most of the studios we worked with were still testing their games — none had something fully developed or market-ready. Their focus was tokenomics over gameplay, so when the token economy collapsed, there was little left to keep players engaged.

Learning from failure: five takeaways

1 · Timing is everything

Being early to an industry can be both an advantage and a challenge. We were ahead of the curve in recognizing the potential of Web3 games, but the market wasn't ready — the ecosystem was immature and the infrastructure wasn't strong enough for large-scale adoption. Even a great idea can fail if the timing isn't right.

2 · Focus on core value, not hype

One of our biggest mistakes was getting caught up in the hype around tokens without ensuring the games themselves were fun to play. We should have focused more on helping studios create engaging, sustainable gameplay rather than relying on token incentives. In gaming, content is king.

3 · Market volatility can kill your business model

Building a business model heavily tied to something as unpredictable as cryptocurrency is risky. When the market tanks, so does your revenue. Since then I've learned to diversify revenue streams and avoid relying too heavily on conditions outside my control.

4 · Product-market fit is crucial

We were building something cool, but it didn't have solid product-market fit in the current landscape. Web3 gamers were too focused on earning tokens, and traditional gamers weren't ready to jump into blockchain yet. Understand the market and build for the right audience at the right time.

5 · Perseverance through setbacks

As a founder you're going to hit roadblocks and you're going to fail — that's okay. What matters is how you adapt and learn. Geekzwolf taught me resilience and the need to pivot when things don't go to plan.

The takeaway. Geekzwolf was a bold venture, and though it didn't succeed, it permanently changed how I evaluate a market and a business model: "is the ecosystem ready" is now its own diligence question, separate from "is the idea good." Failure is just another step on the path — these lessons shape how I approach product to this day.

Note: Geekzwolf is included here as an independent venture pursued alongside my core product management career. It isn't listed as a formal role on my CV, but it's some of the most instructive work in this portfolio, so it stays.
